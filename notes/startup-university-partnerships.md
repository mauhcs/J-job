## Two different things that get conflated

- **大学発ベンチャー** — companies *spun out of* a university, founded on a professor's or
  student's invention. MEXT tracks these.
- **Startups partnering with universities** — existing companies buying research, a name, or
  a lab. This is the one relevant to us, and it is counted inside the 共同研究 statistics
  rather than separately.

Keeping them apart matters, because the spin-out numbers are large and tempting and they are
not our market.

## The spin-out ecosystem, for scale (MEXT FY2023)

**5,215 university-origin ventures founded cumulatively; 4,825 still operating, across 222
institutions.** In FY2023, university-affiliated funds made **111 equity investments totalling
¥12.07bn** (national universities 88 / ¥8.68bn; private 23 / ¥3.39bn) — averaging roughly
¥109m per investment.

Universities have built machinery around this: **116 incubation facilities, 114 accelerator
programmes, 82 venture-certification schemes, 82 business-expansion support programmes,
42 mentor systems, and 37 schemes for introducing executive talent** to their ventures. Every
one of those counts rose year on year.

**The one to notice is the last.** 37 institutions run formal mechanisms to introduce
*executives and specialists* into university-origin ventures. That is a named, institutional
channel for someone with an academic affiliation and industry depth to reach startups —
and it is a channel nobody in this plan has approached.

## What the company is actually buying

From the Japanese practitioner literature, four things, in roughly this order:

1. **The professor's name and network.** Stated plainly: university professors carry a track
   record of joint research, strong connections to companies and stakeholders, and name value —
   **"indispensable, particularly when competing on technical strength."** For a startup whose
   claim is technical, the affiliation is the proof.
2. **Access to public money.** Partnering with a university opens 産学連携 grants and subsidies
   that are unavailable to a company alone. **This may be the single most underrated part of
   the proposition:** the collaboration is not only a cost, it is a key to a funding pool.
3. **Research facilities at low cost.**
4. **Actual joint technology development.**

Note what is *not* on the list: rigorous independent assessment. The company is buying
credibility, access and capability — not scrutiny. That is a more honest reading of the demand
than the validation thesis ever had.

## Why this is good news for the profile

The three things a startup wants most — a credentialed name, grant access, and technical depth —
are exactly what an adjunct professor with HFT and risk-modelling experience supplies. None of
them requires the company to believe in model risk management first.

It also reframes the pitch. Not *"let me examine your model"* (scrutiny, which nobody buys),
but *"let us research this together, publish it, and use it"* — which is what the sample in
`note-sangaku-field-evidence` shows companies actually pay for.

## The contracting is a solved problem

The JPO publishes a **model 共同研究開発契約書** specifically for university × university-origin
venture arrangements, clause by clause. The paperwork is standardised, which removes one
objection — though it presumes a Japanese university as counterparty, which returns to
`task-tuj-sangaku-capability`.

## Open questions

- Do the 37 executive-introduction schemes accept outside specialists, or only alumni of that
  university? Worth one enquiry.
- Which grant programmes require a university partner, and what do they pay? If a collaboration
  unlocks a subsidy larger than the research fee, the fee stops being the constraint.
