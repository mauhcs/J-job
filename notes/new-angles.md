The plan so far has been one idea — sell model validation — pushed down-market and then
re-shaped when it did not survive contact. This note does something different: start from
**what the profile uniquely supplies** and **what the market already pays for**, and look for
overlaps that were never considered.

Constraints taken as fixed: cannot run a sales pipeline; two days a week at most; needs the
counterparty to initiate; credibility must come from the affiliation rather than a track
record that does not exist yet.

Three things the profile supplies that almost nobody else in Japan combines: **HFT and
execution-level market microstructure**, **on-chain interest-rate benchmark construction**,
and **a university affiliation**.

---

## 1. Expert witness and litigation support

**The fit.** Expert-witness firms — SEDA Experts, Expert Institute, Cahn Litigation Services —
run practices in exactly the profile's areas: a named **Algorithmic & Electronic Trading**
practice, and quantitative finance experts covering rates, FX, credit, inflation and equity
derivatives including exotics, across Singapore, Hong Kong and London. Experts are appointed
to produce research reports and court evidence in litigation and arbitration from Hong Kong to
the US. Several listed experts hold **faculty positions at Asian universities, including
HKUST** — the academic affiliation is part of the credential, not decoration.

**Why it answers the binding constraint.** You do not find the work; a law firm or an
expert-witness firm finds you, because they are searching for a named person with exactly this
CV. Engagements are episodic, which suits two days a week. Rates in this segment are the
highest in expert services.

**Why it has not been considered.** It is not consulting, so it never came up while the plan
was fixated on selling a validation practice.

**What to check.** Whether these firms take Japan-based experts and for which jurisdictions;
what HFT-related disputes actually arise in this region; and whether IPOR or TUJ constrain it.

---

## 2. Benchmark oversight and independent benchmark assessment

**The fit, and it is unusually exact.** IOSCO Principle 5 requires a benchmark administrator
to maintain an **oversight function** reviewing and challenging every aspect of benchmark
determination, carried out by a separate committee or equivalent independent arrangement, and
composed of a **balanced group of persons**. Separately, the EU Benchmarks Regulation
recognition pathway requires IOSCO compliance to be demonstrated through assessment by an
**independent external auditor**.

So the benchmark world has, written into its rulebook, two paid roles for outsiders:
**independent oversight-committee member**, and **independent assessor of IOSCO compliance**.

**Why the profile fits better than anyone's.** Chief Scientist at a firm that builds on-chain
interest-rate benchmarks is, almost by definition, one of a very small number of people who
understand benchmark methodology *and* hold an academic post. Japan has its own benchmark
administration around TORF and TONA; Hong Kong and Singapore have theirs; and every digital
asset index provider needs the same governance.

**Why it is attractive structurally.** Oversight-committee seats are **recurring, scheduled,
low-hour and prestigious** — the opposite of project consulting. It is the closest thing found
to income that does not require selling and does not consume weeks.

**The catch.** Independence rules may exclude someone affiliated with a competing benchmark
administrator. That has to be checked before it is pursued, not after.

## 3. A 社外取締役 or risk-committee seat at a small listed fintech

**The fit.** Japanese skill-matrix disclosure shows inside directors rarely carry
**risk management, governance, finance or legal** skills, and companies explicitly use outside
directors to fill those gaps. Disclosure practice has spread fast. The published critique is
that skill categories skew to traditional areas — accounting, legal — with **few items covering
digital and new trends**, which is precisely the gap a quantitative and AI-risk specialist fills.

**Why it is worth more than the fee.** A board seat is a standing credential, an inside view of
how a financial company actually makes decisions, and a network — the things the 顧問 was
supposed to supply, obtained by being the specialist rather than by recruiting one.

**What to check.** Compensation for a small-cap outside director in Japan (not found), whether
a foreign national and an adjunct academic is a plausible candidate, and how these seats are
actually filled — `org-pasona-jobhub` places 社外取締役 and is a starting point.

## 4. Paid teaching into financial institutions through きんざい

**The fit.** 金融財政事情研究会 (Kinzai), founded 1950 under the Ministry of Finance, supplies
training, e-learning and certification to Japanese financial institutions across risk
management among other fields, and runs **more than 1,000 instructor-days a year** of
講師派遣 — dispatched instructors.

**Why it answers the constraint.** They have the client relationships and they source the
instructors. No pipeline, no procurement, and the subject matter — model risk, AI model
governance under the FSA's discussion papers — is new enough that their catalogue almost
certainly lacks it.

**The compounding part.** Teaching risk officers at banks and regional institutions *is*
business development for every other line in this plan, and it is paid rather than costing a
day. It also produces the material for the flagship paper as a by-product.

**What to check.** Whether they engage non-Japanese instructors, and what they pay.

---

## 5. Write the AI model validation methodology, then sell training rather than engagements

**The fit.** Three regulators now require independent validation of AI-driven models and none
specifies how (`note-ai-validation-gap`). Searching found **no published methodology and no
published effort data** (`note-deliverable-spec`). That is a vacuum, and vacuums in
methodology get filled by whoever writes first.

**The move.** Do not sell bespoke validations at the start. **Publish the protocol** — arXiv,
then the Journal of Risk Model Validation — and then monetise the position it creates through
training, a short course, and the inbound that a cited methodology produces. The Big 4 will
adopt whatever standard exists, which makes the author the person they call.

**Why it suits the constraints better than consulting.** One artefact, written once, working
continuously, with no pipeline. It is the only item here that compounds.

**The honest risk.** Long. Twelve to twenty-four months before it pays anything, and it may be
scooped. It should run underneath one of the cash-generating items above, not instead of them.

## Ranking against the constraints

| angle | selling required | time shape | pays now | compounds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expert witness | none — they find you | episodic | high rate | reputation only |
| Benchmark oversight | one introduction | recurring, low hours | modest, recurring | yes |
| Outside director seat | one introduction | recurring, low hours | modest, recurring | strongly |
| Kinzai teaching | none — they source | scheduled days | modest | yes, as marketing |
| Write the standard | none | self-paced | no | strongly |

**What I would drop:** the independent validation practice as a *primary* business. It needs a
credential you do not yet have, an entity you may not be able to form, and a demand premise
that remains unverified. It is the destination, and four of the five items above are better
roads to it than trying to sell it directly.

**The through-line.** Every angle here monetises *being a credentialed independent expert*
rather than *running a consultancy*. That is a different business, it suits the constraints,
and it is what the profile actually is.
