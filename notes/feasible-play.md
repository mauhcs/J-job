**Revised 2026-09-22 after the numbers in it were challenged and did not survive.**

The worry: *business development at four tiers, plus marketing and exposure, plus doing the
analysis and reporting — that is three jobs, and one contract at a time would have to be
enormous to justify it.*

The original version of this note answered that with a revenue model built on fabricated fees
(`note-correction-invented-numbers`) and treated the sales problem as solvable by structure —
"the 顧問 does the BD", "publishing is the marketing". Both of those take a year to work and
neither says what to do on Monday, so neither is an answer to *selling takes forever and I do
not know how*.

**The revised position: treat the inability to sell as a fixed constraint.** The feasible
business is then not an independent practice but being the specialist that firms with mandates
call — subcontracting, expert networks, a domestic boutique partnership. That is
`note-sales-routes`, and it supersedes most of what follows.

The points below still hold as far as they go, with that correction in front of them.

## 1. You are not unemployed

This is the reframe that dissolves most of the problem. You hold two positions — Chief
Scientist at IPOR Labs and Adjunct Assistant Professor at TUJ. This does not have to replace
them, and probably should not.

**At two days a week, 70–80 billable days a year at ¥400k is ¥28–32m.** That is a serious
second income from a practice that is *designed* to be part-time, with the other three days
genuinely available for the BD and publishing that a full-time solo practice cannot fund.

The failure mode is not "too small to be worth it". It is treating this as a full practice
that must carry all overhead from day one.

## 2. Publishing is not a third job — it is the marketing

This was a framing error in how the plan was originally laid out, and it inflated the
workload by a whole stream. The paper, the JAFEE presentation, and the CFA Society talk are
not separate from business development. **They are business development** — the highest-
leverage form available, because they work while you are doing something else and they move
you across the ten-fold rate gap in `note-unit-economics`.

One flagship piece a year, reused as a talk, a preprint and a one-pager, is the entire
marketing function. Budget it as such. Do not also plan outbound.

## 3. The 顧問 exists precisely to do the part you cannot

Business development is the function being outsourced. That is what the role is for, and it
is why `task-komon-comp-structure` matters more than the candidate shortlist: on a success
fee, a 顧問 costs nothing until they produce, and produces exactly the thing you have no time
to manufacture — warm introductions.

Match them to one tier, not four.

## 4. VisasQ is business development that pays you

`org-visasq` was carded as market research. It is also the only BD channel that is inbound,
paid, and billed in one-hour units. An hour with an institution that came looking for you is
worth more than a day of outbound, and it pays a few hundred dollars instead of costing a day.

## 5. Delivery leverage: productise, then subcontract

The first validation of a model class takes 15–20 days. The fifth should take 8, because the
protocol, the test suite and the report structure are already written. **That compounding is
the only leverage a solo operator gets without hiring**, and it argues for going deep in one
or two model classes rather than taking whatever arrives.

Beyond that, the structure is visible in `org-finalyse` — quantitative modellers supported by
BI analysts, engineers and DBAs. You do not need employees to copy it: data preparation,
replication and documentation can go to a subcontractor or to one of the Japanese ALM
boutiques in `org-jp-risk-boutiques`, who bring domestic contracting and language as well.
You keep the senior judgement, which is the part that is actually you.

## 6. The tiers are a sequence, not a portfolio

The Targets view reads as four markets to be worked at once. It is not. It is an order:
startup → SME → mid → large. **Each tier's reference case is what unlocks the next**, and you
work one at a time. There is no year in which BD runs at four tiers simultaneously.

## What this implies for the shape of the offer

Sell **retainers, not projects**, wherever the buyer will take one. A project ends and the
business development treadmill restarts; a retainer is the same revenue without the
re-selling, and it matches what the SME tier actually needs — not one report but an ongoing
function they cannot staff. Fractional executive benchmarks sit at $3–15k/month for standard
engagements; a Japanese 顧問 retainer is ¥200–350k/month. **A "model risk function as a
service" retainer at ¥300–500k/month, with three or four clients, is ¥11–24m/year of
predictable revenue on a manageable and schedulable load** — and it is a far better fit for a
part-time practice than chasing one large project at a time.

## The honest risk

None of this is proven. The rate is triangulated from adjacent markets, not observed in
Japan; the retainer model assumes buyers will take one; and the whole thing assumes somebody
pays for independent validation at the SME tier at all, which `task-garage-bank-conversation`
is designed to test cheaply.

**The correct next move is not to build the practice. It is to sell one engagement**, at any
tier, at a real price, and see what actually happens. One completed piece of paid work will
teach more about feasibility than any further planning — and it is a small enough commitment
that it does not require answering the capacity question first.
