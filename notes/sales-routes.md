The unaddressed problem: selling takes forever, and there is no knowledge of which companies
to approach, how to approach them, or what to say. Structural aphorisms — "the 顧問 does the
BD", "publishing is the marketing" — do not answer it, because both take a year to work and
neither tells you what to do on Monday.

**Treat the inability to sell as a fixed constraint, not a skill gap to be fixed.** Routes
then rank by how much selling each requires, and the top of the list is where the answer is.

## Route A — deliver for people who already sold the work (least selling)

The firms in `note-competitive-landscape` have the mandates, the client relationships and the
procurement approvals. What they periodically lack is a specialist who can tear down a pricing,
execution or microstructure model. **That gap is the product, and selling into it is one
conversation with a practice lead rather than a campaign.**

This is a real, named channel, not a hope:

- **PwC Talent Exchange** and **Deloitte Open Talent** are the firms' own subcontractor
  platforms. **You do not have to be an alumnus to register.** Large firms manage consultants
  in a three-tier panel: global providers for high-stakes work, regional specialists, and a
  local tier of boutiques and individual experts for targeted work. The third tier is the one
  to enter.
- The same applies to the Japanese ALM/risk boutiques in `org-jp-risk-boutiques` — they have
  domestic contracting, language and client relationships and may lack deep quant modelling.
  They also solve the foreign-principal problem (`task-jp-foreign-validator-legality`).

**The published caveat is worth taking seriously:** subcontractors are hired more readily
when their skill plugs into large multi-person projects than when it is a solo activity, so
position as "the quant specialist who slots into your validation workstream", not as a rival
practice. And the standard warning — diversify; a subcontracting relationship is a client
concentration, and the firm owns the end client.

Rate will be lower than a direct engagement. That is the trade: you exchange margin for not
having to sell, which is exactly the right trade when selling is the binding constraint.

## Route B — inbound, paid by the hour (no selling, no pipeline)

Expert networks. **GLG, AlphaSights, Guidepoint, Tegus** collectively run tens of thousands
of expert sessions a month; experts are typically paid **$300–500/hour** while the networks
charge clients $500–2,000. `org-visasq` is the Japanese equivalent with Tokyo, Hong Kong and
Singapore offices.

The counterparty initiates, so there is no pipeline to build and no approach to design.

**What is unknown, and must not be assumed:** no data was found on how many calls an
individual advisor actually receives per month. Volume depends on profile, seniority and
availability. **Do not model income from this** — register, see what arrives over three
months, and then know. It costs an afternoon to find out.

Its real value is probably not the fee anyway. It is that every call is a paid conversation
with someone in the buyer's seat, which is the market research the desk work could not reach.

## Route C — one warm contact, one engagement (a little selling)

`org-garage-bank` — a former employer. One conversation, no pipeline, no cold approach.
The purpose is not revenue; it is to find out what a validation actually takes
(`note-deliverable-spec`) and whether anyone in that world has ever been asked for one.

## Route D — everything the plan previously assumed (the most selling)

Cold outbound to the SME and mid tiers, 顧問-brokered introductions, publishing-led inbound.
Long, uncertain, and dependent on a demand premise that was never verified. **This is the part
that was written up as the plan, and it should be the last part attempted, not the first.**

## The conclusion this points to

If selling is the binding constraint and it is not going to be relieved, then **the feasible
business is not an independent validation practice. It is being the specialist that firms
with mandates call** — subcontracting, expert networks, and a partner relationship with a
domestic boutique — with a direct practice as an option that opens later if reference cases
and the publishing record accumulate.

That is a smaller, less romantic business. It is also one that can start next month, requires
no 顧問, no entity, no positioning decision and no paper, and it produces the two things
currently missing: **an observed price and an observed effort**.
