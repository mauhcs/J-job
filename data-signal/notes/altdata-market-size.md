## Ignore the market-size reports

Published global alternative data forecasts, all found in one sweep:

| source | 2026 | terminal |
|---|---|---|
| A | $29.6bn | $276.9bn by 2033 (37.6% CAGR) |
| B | $31.86bn | **$1,696.89bn by 2034** |
| C | — | ~$23.1bn by 2030 |

**These disagree by roughly two orders of magnitude.** One forecast has the market reaching
1.7 trillion dollars, which would make alternative data larger than the entire global asset
management fee pool. Vendor-sponsored market-sizing reports are marketing collateral. **Do not
quote any of these numbers**, and be suspicious of anyone who does.

## Use the demand-side surveys instead

These are specific, recent and attributable, and they say something useful:

- A survey of firms managing about **$2.4 trillion in AUM** found **94% expect to increase
  alternative data spending in 2026**, and **18% expect a substantial rise**.
- **54% said their budgets for buying and managing alternative data grew by 50% or more over
  the past two years.**
- Firms subscribe to an average of **~20 alternative datasets**.
- Hedge fund operators were the largest end-user segment at **67.66% of revenue share** in 2025.

That is a market where the buyers are committed and spending is compounding — which is what
matters, rather than the absolute size.

## The supply-side number that actually shapes the opportunity

Eagle Alpha catalogues **1,200+ sources** and forecasts **5,000 within two years**.

**This is the single most important fact in this space.** If supply roughly quadruples while
buyers subscribe to ~20 datasets each, then the binding constraint stops being the existence of
data and becomes **attention and proof**. Thousands of vendors will be competing for evaluation
slots at funds that can only assess a handful of datasets a quarter.

In that world the scarce good is a **credible, independent reason to believe a specific dataset
matters** — which is the product. The supply glut is the business case, and it is better
evidence than any market-size forecast.
