Two questions: what the money looks like, and who is already doing this so it can be joined
rather than competed with.

## The numbers that let a price be derived rather than guessed

- **A dataset subscription starts around $25,000–$40,000 a year** for an enterprise engagement.
- **Fund budgets** split roughly evenly across under $250k, $250k–$1m, and $1m–$5m a year.
  The $250k–$1m band is just under **28%** of the reported market; only about **5%** spend
  $5m–$10m.
- Buyers subscribe to about **20 datasets** a year, with reported answers running from 1 to
  over 100.

**The derivation, labelled as mine.** If one dataset earns $25–40k per client per year, then a
vendor who closes three extra subscriptions on the back of a credible report has gained
**$75–120k of recurring revenue**. A fee of **$10,000–25,000 for an evaluation report** is
therefore a fraction of the first year of what it unlocks, and can be argued from the vendor's
own economics rather than from a day rate.

That is the right way to price this, and it is the opposite of what was done in the other
project — the fee follows from the buyer's revenue, not from a guess about effort. It still
has to survive an effort measurement, which is what step 1 of `note-altdata-action-plan` is for.

## Five ways it gets paid for

1. **Fixed fee per dataset evaluation.** Simplest, anchored as above. Repeatable once the
   harness exists.
2. **Tiered: cheap screen, then full evaluation.** A low-price integrity-and-viability screen
   that most datasets fail, with the full study only for survivors. Matches how vendors triage
   and lowers the entry price to something a small vendor will try.
3. **Retainer with a multi-dataset vendor.** Recurring, schedulable — the shape this profile
   most needs.
4. **Success fee or revenue share** on subscriptions closed. Perfectly aligned and very hard
   to verify from outside; treat as a sweetener, not a base.
5. **Subcontracting to the firms that already sell this** — the route requiring no selling at
   all, and the answer to the second question.

## Who already sells it, and could be approached

| firm | what they do | why they might use you |
|---|---|---|
| **Neudata Consulting** | Explicitly sells data monetisation consulting; analysts specialised by sector, dataset type and **geography** | A bench organised by specialism uses outsiders when a brief falls outside it — Japan, non-equity, microstructure |
| **Eagle Alpha** | Data strategy consulting; keeps **data scientists on staff** to clean and structure datasets | Same shape, already staffing this skill |
| **Amass Insights** | Facilitator; publishes on sourcing and **monetization strategies** | Smaller, so more likely to use specialists than hire them |
| **Exabel** | The platform competitor | Platforms need bespoke analyst work around the edges of what they automate |
| **M Science** | ~120 people selling **research derived from data**, 3,922 companies covered since 2002 | A research firm has analyst work; its coverage is US-centric |
| **Toptal** | Freelance marketplace with a quantitative analyst category | Inbound, low effort; a sensor for what firms actually ask a freelance quant for |

**The important reframe.** Neudata selling consulting is not bad news. It is proof the service
is bought, by a firm with the deepest relationships on both sides — and **being their
specialist for the briefs their bench cannot cover is a faster route to paid work than
competing with them for the same vendors.** It is the same conclusion reached in the other
project about subcontracting, arrived at independently, which is some evidence it is right for
this profile rather than a coincidence of one market.

## What still has to be checked

- Do these firms use external specialists at all, or staff everything? **Ask them.**
- What do they pay a contract analyst, and is it above the contractor floor?
- Is there enough Japan or non-equity work to be the specialism, or is that a niche of a niche?
