## The numbers

From CESA's own survey of Japanese game developers:

- **85.8% use generative AI** at least occasionally — **63% daily**, 22.8% occasionally.
- **Up from 51% the previous year.**
- **32% of CESA member companies** use AI to help develop in-house game engines.
- Most common applications, in order: **visual and image generation**, then story and text
  generation, then programming support.

For contrast, a GDC 2026 survey put global industry use of generative AI tools at around 36%.
The definitions differ, so the gap is not directly comparable — but nothing suggests Japanese
studios are behind, and the CESA figure suggests the opposite.

## What this means for a service business — and it is not what it looks like

**The bull reading is wrong.** 85.8% adoption does not mean 85.8% of studios are shopping for
AI help. It means the easy, visible work — image generation, text drafts, coding assistance —
is already being done in-house with off-the-shelf tools. A consultant arriving to offer
generative AI is arriving a year late to a market that solved it with a subscription.

**The useful reading:** adoption this fast and this broad normalises the category. Budgets
exist, internal advocates exist, and nobody has to be convinced that ML belongs in a studio.
The remaining question is **which problems are still open because they need a skill the studio
does not have** — and those are unlikely to be generative.

Note also what is *absent* from the list of common applications: nothing about cheat detection,
economy modelling, churn, LTV or matchmaking. Either those are handled elsewhere in the
organisation and not counted as "generative AI use", or they are under-served. Worth
establishing which (`task-game-survey-usecases`).
