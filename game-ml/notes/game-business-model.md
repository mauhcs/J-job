## The product ladder

Three things, ordered by how much trust each requires:

1. **Diagnostic** — a fixed-scope, fixed-fee study of one system. Verify gacha disclosure
   against implementation and logs; or assess an existing cheat-detection pipeline's false
   positive and false negative behaviour. Weeks, bounded, low access requirement, produces a
   document. **This is the wedge**: a studio can say yes without restructuring anything.
2. **Build** — develop the detection or estimation system. Months, deep access, real fees, and
   only available after a diagnostic has built trust.
3. **Retained** — ongoing monitoring as the adversary adapts. Cheat detection decays by
   definition, so this one is natural rather than invented, and it is the only shape that
   produces recurring revenue.

## Pricing

**Not yet.** The lesson from the other projects is that a fee table invented before an effort
measurement does not survive contact. What can be said: Japanese custom-AI contractors take
small and mid-sized projects, so the market is not exclusively enterprise-scale — the price
floor is set by what Laboro.AI or Ridge-i would quote for a comparable scope, and that is
findable by asking them (`task-game-approach-vendors`).

## Distribution — four routes, ranked by how much selling each requires

**1. CEDEC, and it is the strongest route here.** Japan's largest game developers' conference,
run by CESA, held annually — 2026 was 22–24 July in Yokohama. It has an **open session
submission process**, and the 2026 programme included mathematical optimisation and machine
learning for multi-player imperfect-information game AI, so quantitative material is in scope.

A CEDEC talk is the track record, the credential and the discovery channel in one artefact,
obtained by submitting rather than by selling. **It is the closest thing to a solved
distribution problem in any of the three projects** — and the same conclusion, again: publish
something and let the audience come.

**2. Subcontracting to domestic AI vendors.** Laboro.AI, Ridge-i, AVILEN, and the research
ventures. They own the client relationships and their benches are CV/NLP-shaped. Same route
that both other projects converged on.

**3. University research collaboration.** Square Enix partnered with the University of Tokyo's
Matsuo-Iwasawa lab on QA automation; the University of Tokyo runs a game AI research group.
Japanese game companies demonstrably do 産学連携 with AI labs — a documented, repeated
behaviour, which is better evidence than any buyer thesis in the other projects. The TUJ
affiliation is the entry, and the earlier work on 産学連携 contract mechanics applies directly.

**4. Direct approach to studios.** Last. Slowest, most gated, and the thing that has failed
three times.

## What would make this work when the others did not

One difference is real: **this industry has a public, open-submission conference whose audience
is exactly the buyer.** Neither of the other projects had that. Model validation had JAFEE,
which is academic and adjacent; alt data had a podcast, which required being interesting to a
host first.

CEDEC accepts submissions on merit. That converts "how do I get discovered" from a
relationship problem into a writing problem — which is the one thing that has consistently
been within reach.
